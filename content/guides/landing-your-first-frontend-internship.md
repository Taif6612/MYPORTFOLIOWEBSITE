The first internship is the hardest because you're trying to prove experience
you don't have yet. The fix isn't more credentials — it's evidence and signal.

## Learn the things teams actually use

You don't need to know everything. You need to be genuinely solid on a small,
high-leverage core:

- **HTML & CSS** — semantics, flexbox, grid, responsive layout, accessibility.
- **JavaScript** — the language, not just a framework. Async, the DOM, fetch.
- **One framework, deeply** — React is the safest bet for most listings.
- **Git** — branches, commits, pull requests. See the [Git guide](/guides/git-and-github-for-students).

### Depth beats breadth

A candidate who knows React *well* is more hireable than one who has touched
React, Vue, Svelte, and Angular but can't explain re-renders.

## Build proof, not just skills

Reviewers trust what they can click. Before you apply, have:

1. A deployed portfolio with two or three real projects.
2. A GitHub profile with consistent, readable commits.
3. One project that solves a problem *you* actually had.

## Apply like it's a numbers game with quality control

Volume matters, but a tailored application converts far better than a generic
blast. For each role:

```md
- Read the job description; mirror its language in your message.
- Reference one specific thing about the product or team.
- Link the single most relevant project, not all of them.
```

> Referrals convert several times better than cold applications. Before you
> submit, ask: do I know anyone one hop away from this team?

## Interview like an engineer, not a student

Most internship interviews test three things:

- **Fundamentals** — can you reason about code out loud?
- **Communication** — do you ask clarifying questions before coding?
- **Curiosity** — do you care about *why*, not just *what*?

When you get stuck, narrate your thinking. Interviewers are hiring a future
teammate, and they're listening for how you'd work through a real problem
together.

## After the interview

Send a short, specific thank-you. If you're rejected, ask for one piece of
feedback and act on it. Most successful candidates didn't get the first offer —
they got the *fifth*, after four rounds of iteration.
